Why it can't fail

Proof, three ways.

A guarantee needs a reason. Below: the statement, an induction you can step through, a loop-invariant version, an existence argument that works even without knowing the strategy, and a brute-force check that doesn't use the formula at all.

Theorem

For every number of prizes n ≥ 1, Amanda can guarantee the book. If 7 divides n she lets Bodil start; otherwise she starts and takes n mod 7. Afterwards she answers Bodil's take k with 7 − k (or takes everything when at most 6 remain).

Set-up and reduction

  1. Positions. The state of the game is fully described by r, the number of prizes still in the line, plus whose turn it is. Which prizes remain is forced: they are prizes 1…r.

  2. The book. Prize 1 is only reachable when the line has ≤ 6 prizes and the mover takes all of them. So the player who makes the last take gets the book.

  3. Winning and losing positions. Call r a winning position if the player about to move from r can force making the last take, and a losing position if the opponent can. Position 0 is losing for the player to move: the previous player just took the last prize.

  4. Goal. Show r is losing exactly when 7 | r. Amanda then picks the role that is not losing.

1 · Strong induction (step through it)

Claim. For every r ≥ 0: the player to move at r loses ⇔ 7 | r.

winning (N)losing (P)

  1. Base. r = 0 is losing (nobody can move; the last take was made by the opponent), and 7 | 0.

  2. Hypothesis. Suppose the claim holds for all positions below some r ≥ 1.

  3. Case 7 ∤ r. Let s = r mod 7, so 1 ≤ s ≤ 6. The mover may legally take s, reaching r − s, a multiple of 7 below r. By hypothesis that position is losing for the next player. So r is winning.

  4. Case 7 | r (r ≥ 7). Any legal take is some k ∈ {1,…,6}. It leads to r − k ≡ −k ≢ 0 (mod 7), which by hypothesis is a winning position for the opponent. Every move loses, so r is losing.

∎

2 · The invariant version

Instead of classifying every position, just follow Amanda's play and watch what stays true.

  1. Setup.

    If 7 ∤ n, Amanda's opening take makes the line a multiple of 7. If 7 | n, it already is, and Bodil moves.

  2. Invariant.

    Before each of Bodil's turns, the remaining count is a positive multiple of 7.

  3. Preserved.

    Bodil takes k ∈ [1, 6]. Amanda takes 7 − k ∈ [1, 6] (always legal and always possible, since at least 7 − k remain). Together: exactly 7 gone, so the count is a multiple of 7 again.

  4. Termination.

    The count strictly decreases, so it reaches 0. It reaches 0 on Amanda's move, since before Bodil's turn it is always ≥ 7 > 6, so she can never empty the line.

In one line

Bodil never faces ≤ 6

The invariant says Bodil always faces a multiple of 7, so at least 7. She can't clear it. Amanda eventually faces ≤ 6 and finishes. The book follows.

3 · An existence argument (no arithmetic needed)

There is a slicker reason that Amanda can win every n, which does not mention 7.

Zermelo's theorem (for this game)

The game is finite, has perfect information, no chance, and no draws (someone makes the last take). Then from the starting position, exactly one of the two players — the first or the second — has a strategy that guarantees a win.

Amanda chooses who starts. So she simply takes whichever role has the winning strategy. She can therefore guarantee the book for every n, even before finding the strategy.

Why this matters

Existence vs. recipe

This argument proves the answer is all n immediately. The mod-7 analysis supplies the recipe — which role, and which takes — that the task actually asks for. Together: the reason, and the method.

It also explains why this works for any take limit, not just 6. See Generalize.

Spot the flaw

“Always take 3”

“Take 3 every turn. Then the line shrinks by 3 each time, so I'll hit the end exactly.”

Bodil doesn't have to cooperate. If she takes 1 instead of 3, the count mod 3 no longer lines up. The strategy must adapt to her move. The “7 − k” reply does exactly that, for every k.

“Take the biggest”

“Always take 6. That leaves the opponent the smallest chance.”

Try n = 7 as the first player: take 6, leave 1, opponent takes the book. Greed ignores the leftover count. What matters is what remainder you leave, which must be a multiple of 7.

“Go first, it's an advantage”

“Starting is always best: you get the first pick of prizes.”

For n = 7, 14, 21… going first is losing. The first player is forced to break the multiple of 7. That's why Amanda is given the choice: she picks the right side.

“Seven only works for small n”

“For huge n the pattern must break somewhere.”

The induction has no size limit: each position only depends on the six before it, and the same two-case argument repeats forever. Also try the brute-force check up to two million.
